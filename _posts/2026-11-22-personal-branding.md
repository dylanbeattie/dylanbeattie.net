I’ve been playing international conference pinball the last two weeks - Prague for Update Conf, then the direct train to Berlin (A+++ would train again!) for Software Architecture Gathering, and then straight to Thessaloniki for the tenth anniversary of VoxxedDays. It’s been great fun, in that relentless way that conference travel often is - early mornings, late nights, lots of social interaction, lots of low-level logistics like finding places to eat and making sure you’re in the right room at the right time with the right slide deck ready to go. And now I have one of those slightly odd days where hotel checkout is 11:00 and my flight home doesn’t leave until 22:05 and so I spend the day as a sort of very temporary vagabond, sightseeing and drinking coffee and generally chilling out without actually having anywhere to be or anything to do. 

_(This is actually a lie, I have a RIDICULOUS amount of stuff to do, but very little of it is the kind of work one can constructively do in the coffee shops of downtown Thessaloniki on a bright Sunday afternoon.)_

Alongside the regular conference talks and panels, yesterday I did a “mentoring session” with [Josh Long](https://joshlong.com/) about “entrepreneurial personal branding”. I know Josh by reputation, but hadn’t really met him properly until yesterday, and with very little direction from the organisers about what they were looking for, we expected maybe a dozen people, and were a little surprised when more than fifty turned up. So, we asked the room what they were hoping to get out of the session… turns out none of them really knew either, so we just started chatting about all the stuff we’d learned over the years about becoming recognisable in a way that transcends job titles and employers, and it actually ended up being a really fun session.

I think there is a hardline capitalist interpretation of “entrepreneurial personal branding” which is “figure out how to get paid just to be yourself” - and there’s an entire generation of social media influencers out there who would have you believe that this is exactly what they’re doing - but of course, the moment you start doing something just because you’re getting paid, you start to compromise your authenticity.

I have it on good authority that I have a very strong personal brand, but believe me, very little of that is premeditated. I think it’s more of an emergent property; at various points over the years, I’ve got carried away with projects that combine software, technology and rock music in unexpected ways Some of those projects ended up getting a lot of attention - probably because, at the time, I was just having a stupid amount of fun working on them, and audiences, whether online or in-person, generally respond well to that sort of authentic enthusiasm — most notably the Rockstar esoteric language, which started out as a joke in a pub and nearly a decade later I’ve just implemented syntax highlighting for it in time for this year’s Advent of Code. I often get invitations to revisit those projects - I think I’ll be giving variations on my keynote talk “How to be a Rockstar Developer” until I’m dead - and there have definitely been occasions where my heart just wasn’t in it that day, but that’s professionalism: you turn up, you do the talk, you play the music, you chat to the attendees afterwards.

We had a really interesting chat about this sort of “emergent branding” - I know a lot of folks who started out without any plan to establish any kind of brand, but then after a while they realised all their best talks happened to be when they were wearing a particular jacket, or that attendees recognised them by their distinctive Hawaiian shirts, and it became part of their “ritual”, until you realise it’s got out of hand when you find yourself freaking out ‘cos you’ve got to give a talk and you can’t find your lucky shoes.

There’s also quite a nice angle (for me, anyway) in that my particular non-tech domain - rock music - provides some really interesting scenarios for talking about software engineering. A band going on a European tour, selling tickets in a dozen currencies and playing shows in five different timezones, very quickly gets you into all sorts of gnarly problems involving concurrency and normalisation and data formats: there’s enough complexity to be genuinely educational, but it’s also a domain that anybody who’s ever bought a concert ticket online can relate to, and there’s a lot more character and personality in the scenarios than there is in something like motor insurance or bank loans.

And then, after spending forty minutes telling a group of attendees that I didn’t really know what personal branding was, and folks like me aren’t actually famous, we’re just nerds who like to talk about what we do, I was sitting outside a restaurant having dinner with Sander Hoogendoorn and Jules May, when a total stranger came up to our table and asked if I was Dylan Beattie, and told me how much they love my talks and my videos and would he be able to get a selfie with me? (And no, they had no idea VoxxedDays was happening in their home town, or that I was one of the keynote speakers. Marketing is HARD in the age of social media and wall-to-wall distraction machines.)

And so time for a beer and a little light blogging before I head to the airport, a week at home, a Little Angels gig next Sunday night, and then off to Vilnius for Build Stuff. See some of you there, I hope.
